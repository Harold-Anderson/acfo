Last week Anderson CFO sponsored an investor meeting arranged by the [Oregon Entrepreneurs Network](https://oen.org). OEN prepares young companies for success by educating them about business basics. Investors are drawn to the idea that these companies may avoid some of the usual blunders. Indeed, OEN even provides funding to the best companies to go through its programs.

It is a treat to be able to meet the winning companies, who usually have many of the qualities discussed in the other articles in this blog.

However, meeting the investors is the other side of the coin. They are the ones taking the risk. Investing in startups is very risky. I spoke with several investors who bemoaned the extreme lack of preparation he had seen while [watching companies pitch](https://acfo.co/008-how-you-show-up-is-important).

One investor admitted to being rather new to the idea of startup investing. He asked me what was the biggest mistake that companies make after receiving funding.

There are so many fatal mistakes, it took a minute to answer. Finally I said that losing money is a warning sign. It seems obvious, but deserves more examination.

Many successful companies never take investor cash. They were profitable from the beginning. Bootstrapping your business was the only path until recently. Venture capital and angels are relatively new phenomena. If you lost money, it was proof that your business could not build or buy a product and sell it for more.

The idea of venture capital is that for some businesses, it would be advantageous to temporarily lose money in pursuit of faster growth, for instance by hiring a salesperson.

Venture capital can also be used to fund capital investment, such as inventory or a plant. This is technically not losing money. Amazon both lost money and consumed cash building warehouses for many years.

These cases are exceptions, however. For most businesses, if they cannot make money immediately, this is a warning sign. The next step would be determining the cause of the loss.

Having now advised dozens of companies over many years, and at the risk of stating the obvious, here are the major categories that may require intervention by the investor:

**Spending too much** Most companies would do well to simply create a balanced budget and stick to it every month or quarter at the outside. If the company loses money versus the budget, that must be adjusted.

Again, this seems obvious, but I have had clients lose money consistently for years and finance it with debt. This eventually results in a death spiral where the company sinks under debt service payments. Recovering from this requires a capital infusion and a balanced budget.

**Insufficient Sales** Every financial plan I have ever seen has been too optimistic. Usually sales come in much less than anticipated. While expenses and capital investment can be predicted, sales may not be under your control to the same degree. The result again is a loss. When this occurs, the company must first adjust expenses to match the income and then ask why the market uptake is not as expected.

I cannot stress this highly enough. Most founders overestimate the appeal of their products, expecting that consumers will value the unique selling points that led them to create the product in the first place. This is rarely the case. Lack of product-market fit is by far the leading cause of business failure.

**Prices too high or too low** Sometimes companies price themselves out of the market. This happens when someone (nameless) tells them they need a certain gross margin. If their cost structure is high, the resulting price is not attractive to customers. Sales decline, which results in even higher losses.

If prices are too low, sales may be good, but you can't lose money on every sale and make it up on volume.

Pricing must start at the shelf, working backwards to find the necessary cost of goods. If you can't meet that cost of goods, don't enter the market.

**Summary** There are other pitfalls, such as binding the company with fixed expenses when demand can be variable, but this is a special case of what we have already discussed.

Good management interprets losses as a signal to adjust course immediately. Companies of every size need to keep a watchful eye on their bottom line, all the time.
